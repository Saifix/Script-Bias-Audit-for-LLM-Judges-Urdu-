# Is the AI Judge Fair to Roman Urdu?
### Measuring script bias in LLM judges

Large language models are increasingly used as **judges**: they grade answers, rank chatbot replies and decide
which model output is "better". Urdu has roughly 230 million speakers, and online many of them write it in **Roman script**
(Latin letters, e.g. *"do kilo seb"*) rather than the standard Perso-Arabic **Urdu script** (*"دو کلو سیب"*),
and they freely mix in English words (*"do kilo apples"*).

If an LLM judge gives a lower score to the *same correct answer* just because it is written in Roman Urdu, every
evaluation, leaderboard and reward model built on that judge quietly penalises how Urdu speakers actually write.
This project measures whether that happens, how large the effect is, and whether simple fixes remove it.

> **Research question.** Holding content fixed, does the script an answer is written in — Urdu script, Roman Urdu,
> English, or Urdu–English code-mixed — change how an LLM judge scores, compares and understands it?

---

## What we test

Every question exists in **four versions**; a judge always sees the passage, question and answer in the same version:

| Version | Example (the same answer) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Urdu script** (reference) | دو کلو سیب | Belebele `urd_Arab` |
| **Roman Urdu** | do kilo seb | Answers: word-by-word transliteration of the Urdu script by Claude Opus 5.5 (high effort). Passage and question: Belebele `urd_Latn` |
| **English** | two kilos of apples | Belebele `eng_Latn` |
| **Code-mixed** | do kilo apples | Answers: Roman Urdu with selected nouns switched to English, by Claude Opus 5.5 (high effort). Passage and question: Belebele `urd_Latn` |

Why we wrote our own Roman Urdu answers: a pilot found that about 9 of 20 of Belebele's own Roman Urdu answer options
were **reworded rather than transliterated** (e.g. "Apple" became "Seeb"), which would mix up *script* effects with
*wording* effects. Our transliteration keeps every word, so only the script changes.

## Method

**Data.** 300 reading-comprehension items sampled (fixed seed) from **Belebele** (Bandarkar et al.), which provides
the same passage, question and four answer options in Urdu script, Roman Urdu and English. For each item we use the
correct option and one wrong option. The passage and question are always shown in the same script as the answer. All
300 items have a code-mixed version following the noun-switching approach of *Lost in the Mix*
(Mohamed et al., ACL 2026).

**Generated versions — provenance.** The Roman Urdu transliterations and the code-mixed versions (300 items × 4
options each) were **generated by Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic) at high reasoning effort**, using
written rules: transliterate word by word without translating or rewording ("seb", never "apple"), common Pakistani
Roman-Urdu spellings, standard English spelling for foreign names; for code-mixing, keep Urdu grammar, verbs and
function words and switch only selected nouns to English. Outputs were checked automatically (four non-empty options
per item, no Urdu script left) and accepted **without human review**; every row in the database records the generator
and an "auto-approved" reviewer label. A human spot-check of a random sample is recommended before the results are published.

**Judges.** Three small open-weights LLMs from three different companies, accessed through OpenRouter:
**Qwen 3.5 9B** (Alibaba), **Llama 3.1 8B Instruct** (Meta) and **Gemma 3 12B** (Google),
at temperature 0 with fixed prompts. The exact model ID, provider and timestamp of every call are recorded.

**Reading the replies.** Judge replies are turned into numbers by plain code (e.g. "Score: 8/10" → 8), never by
another AI. Only afterwards, **Claude Haiku 4.5** (a fourth company's model) writes a one-to-three sentence
plain-English summary from the computed numbers; it never sees or grades the answers themselves.

**Experiments.**

1. **Screening gate** — can the judge tell correct from wrong answers at all (AUC > 0.7 in Urdu script and English)?
   Judges that fail are flagged, because their bias numbers would be noise.
2. **Comprehension** — the judge answers the multiple-choice question itself in each script. Does it *understand*
   Roman Urdu as well as Urdu script?
3. **Pointwise scoring** — "Rate how correct and helpful this answer is from 1 to 10." Same answer, five scripts.
4. **Pairwise comparison** — the same answer in two versions side by side: "Which is better, A or B, or TIE?" Run in
   both orders to cancel position bias. A fair judge says TIE.
5. **Consistency** — repeated scoring on a 50-item subset to separate bias from randomness.
6. **Mitigations** — (a) a rubric line telling the judge to ignore script and spelling, (b) transliterating Roman and
   code-mixed text into Urdu script before judging, (c) averaging a panel of judges.

**Metrics** (per judge × script, Urdu script as reference, bootstrap 95% confidence intervals with 1,000 resamples):

- **Severity gap** — mean score difference on the *same* items (negative = harsher on that script).
- **Error detection** — AUC and separation between correct and wrong answers (does the judge still catch mistakes?).
- **Acceptance rate** — share of answers scored ≥ 7, split by correct and wrong (are wrong Roman Urdu answers waved through?).
- **Pairwise win rate** — ties count half; 50% means no preference.
- **Comprehension accuracy** and **score variance across repeats**.

Ties and unparseable outputs are reported separately, never silently counted as failures.

## Results

Full run on 13 Nov 2026: 300 questions × 4 versions × 3 judges, about 33,000 judge calls in total (cost $0.82).
Numbers in brackets are bootstrap 95% confidence intervals; **bold** = the interval excludes "no difference".
Llama 3.1 8B failed the screening gate (it barely separates right from wrong answers even in Urdu script, AUC 0.65 on
the pilot), so its results are flagged and should not carry the conclusions.

**Screening (can the judge tell right from wrong? AUC, 20-question pilot):** Qwen 0.76 / 0.91, Gemma 0.77 / 0.93,
Llama 0.65 / 0.75 (Urdu script / English). All three are weaker in Urdu script than in English.

| Finding | Metric | Qwen 3.5 9B | Gemma 3 12B | Llama 3.1 8B ⚑ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Correct answers scored lower in Roman Urdu? | score gap vs Urdu script (1–10 scale) | **−0.81** [−1.16, −0.47] | **−0.59** [−0.95, −0.24] | −0.12 [−0.39, +0.15] |
| … and in code-mixed? | score gap vs Urdu script | **−0.91** [−1.27, −0.56] | **−0.86** [−1.22, −0.50] | **−0.31** [−0.57, −0.01] |
| … and in English? | score gap vs Urdu script | +0.34 [−0.06, +0.72] | **+0.57** [+0.19, +0.91] | +0.21 [−0.12, +0.53] |
| Prefers Urdu script head-to-head? | Roman Urdu win rate vs Urdu script (50% = fair) | **13.7%** [12, 15] | **39.5%** [38, 41] | **39.9%** [37, 42] |
| | code-mixed win rate vs Urdu script | **12.3%** [11, 14] | **37.0%** [36, 38] | **40.3%** [38, 43] |
| Understands Roman Urdu as well? | comprehension accuracy: Urdu / Roman / Mixed / English | 76 / 57 / 58 / 92% | 82 / 67 / 69 / 91% | 48 / 33 / 36 / 69% |
| Still catches wrong answers? | AUC: Urdu / Roman / Mixed / English | 0.68 / 0.60 / 0.59 / 0.76 | 0.74 / 0.64 / 0.63 / 0.83 | 0.57 / 0.54 / 0.53 / 0.67 |
| Wrong answers waved through? | share of wrong answers scored ≥ 7: Urdu / Roman | 38% / 41% | 39% / **51%** | 78% / 71% |
| Same score when asked again? | identical score on repeat: Urdu / Roman / Mixed / English | 46 / 50 / 45 / 69% | 77 / 76 / 67 / 79% | 45 / 36 / 32 / 56% |

**Mitigations** (score gap on correct answers vs Urdu script, Roman Urdu / code-mixed):

| | Qwen 3.5 9B | Gemma 3 12B |
|---|---|---|
| No mitigation | **−0.81** / **−0.91** | **−0.59** / **−0.86** |
| Rubric ("ignore script, spelling and formality") | −0.39 / −0.34 | −0.32 / **−0.52** |
| Transliterate to Urdu script first | **+0.07** / −0.32 | −0.27 / **−0.55** |
| Panel of all three judges (averaged) | **−0.51** / **−0.69** (panel) | |

- **Transliterating first works best.** It removes most of the gap *and* restores error detection: Roman Urdu AUC goes
  from 0.60 → 0.68 (Qwen) and 0.64 → 0.72 (Gemma), back to Urdu-script level, and Gemma's wrong-answer acceptance drops
  from 51% to 39%.
- **The rubric shrinks the gap by making judges more lenient**, not fairer: wrong answers scored ≥ 7 rise from 41% to
  59% (Qwen) and 51% to 61% (Gemma) in Roman Urdu. Not a good fix.
- **A panel does not cancel the bias** — all three judges lean the same way, so the average still penalises Roman Urdu
  (−0.51) and code-mixed (−0.69).

**Summary.** Both capable judges (Qwen, Gemma) systematically disadvantage the scripts most Urdu speakers actually use
online: the same correct answer scores lower in Roman Urdu and code-mixed text, loses head-to-head against its Urdu-script
twin, and wrong answers are caught less reliably. Judges also *understand* Roman Urdu less well, and converting it to
Urdu script before judging largely removes the gap — which suggests much of the bias comes from weaker handling of
Roman script rather than a pure stylistic preference.

**Limitations.** Three small open models; a single dataset (Belebele); Roman Urdu and code-mixed answer options were
written by Claude Opus 5.5 and not human-reviewed, while their passages/questions come from Belebele's Roman Urdu;
judges are not fully deterministic at temperature 0 (see the repeat row), so single questions are noisy and only the
300-question averages should be read.

## Why it matters

- **Fairness for real users.** If judges penalise Roman Urdu, chatbots tuned with those judges learn to avoid the way
  most Urdu speakers write, and evaluations under-rate systems that serve them well.
- **Trustworthy evaluation.** Leaderboards and research that use LLM-as-a-judge for South Asian languages need to
  know whether the judge measures quality or script.
- **Practical fixes.** Testing cheap mitigations (a rubric line, transliteration, judge panels) tells practitioners
  what actually works, not just that a problem exists.
- **Reusable method.** The same controlled design — identical content, only the script changes — applies to Hindi
  (Devanagari vs. Hinglish), Arabic (Arabizi), Bengali and other languages written in more than one script.
